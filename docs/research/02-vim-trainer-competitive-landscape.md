# Nobody Has Built keybr for Vim

The gap is real and a solo developer can fill it, but not with ads plus a $1 tier. Across roughly thirty Vim practice products checked on 2026-10-11, **none measures per-skill speed and accuracy over time and uses that measurement to pick the next drill in the browser**, which is the entire keybr loop. The closest are Vim Hero, which shows a per-command level ladder but does not document how it selects practice, and vimhjkl, a terminal tool that schedules by weakness with Leitner boxes but has no charts and no sync. The keybr algorithm itself is four ideas (per-item latency EMA, confidence ratio against a target, an unlock gate, and drill generation biased toward the weakest item) and ports directly to Vim motions. The monetization plan does not survive the numbers: developer traffic is 50–72% ad-blocked, the developer ad networks gate entry at 10k–50k monthly pageviews and pay about $2.50 CPM, so a trainer at 50k pageviews earns on the order of **$60–250 a month**, and a **$1 sale nets $0.45 on a merchant-of-record processor and $0.67 on Stripe**, below Stripe's own $0.50 minimum charge once fees are considered. Nobody in the typing-trainer or Vim-course market sells a ~$1 tier, and the one Vim course with disclosed data (vim.so) found conversion unchanged when it raised its price to $25. The recommended alternative is the only model with precedent in this niche: a free core with no ads in the practice loop, a **one-time $15–25 "pro" purchase** for history, charts, sync and advanced curriculum sold through a merchant of record, and an optional GitHub Sponsors link. Build on CodeMirror 6 plus `@replit/codemirror-vim`, verify pars offline against real Neovim, write exercises fresh or seed from vimhjkl's MIT set, and ship an MVP of 20–30 skills with per-skill charts before adding any payment at all.

## Thirty trainers exist and none charts per-skill speed

The field splits into five shapes. Paid browser courses with a fixed lesson list (Vim Hero, LearnVim, Vimified, Vim Valley, Vimgolf.ai) sit alongside one paid narrative game (Vim Adventures), a crowd of free browser drills (OpenVim, VimMaster, Vim Racer, vimtyper, VimGym, vimchi, vim.is, BobaVim), in-editor Neovim plugins (vim-be-good, hardtime.nvim, precognition.nvim, golf.vim, vimhjkl), and legacy terminal games (PacVim, vimtronner). Only two products do per-skill mastery tracking at all, and only one schedules drills by weakness ([competitors note, GitHub API and site reads](https://github.com/S-Sigdel/vimhjkl)).

**Vim Hero is the closest thing to a keybr for Vim and the main competitor to beat.** Its stats page lists **76 commands in 9 categories**, each with a level and XP and one of four proficiency states (Started, Learning, Practiced, Fluent), and the site claims "Your proficiency in each skill is tracked so you always know where you stand and what to practice next" ([vim-hero.com/stats](https://www.vim-hero.com/stats); [vim-hero.com](https://www.vim-hero.com/)). The practice page offers category tabs and goals/time/endless modes but says nothing about how challenges are chosen, and the XP ladder appears to accrue from completing goals rather than from measured latency or keystroke efficiency ([vim-hero.com/practice](https://www.vim-hero.com/practice)). Pricing is reportedly $20 lifetime after 12 free lessons, though the pricing page returned 404 on the research date so the figure is unverified. Its community footprint is tiny: a 2024 HN submission got 4 points and no comments, and no Reddit threads surfaced ([HN Algolia](https://hn.algolia.com/api/v1/search?query=vim%20hero&tags=story)). Whether its stats page shows historical charts could not be determined without an account.

**vimhjkl is the only product that adapts, and it lives in the terminal.** Each of its **66 skills** uses a Leitner box (levels 1–5) plus a rep counter toward 25, its Practice mode "Focuses on weakest skills with retries until passing", and every one of its 230 challenges is machine-verified against real Vim with a pass requiring correctness and at most 2x the par keystroke count ([vimhjkl README](https://github.com/S-Sigdel/vimhjkl)). It has 544 stars after launching in June 2026 and drew roughly 479 points on r/neovim, the highest-scoring learning-tool post in the earlier research. It has no charts, no accounts, and no sync. Everything else is shallower: Vimified exposes five aggregate numbers with static targets ([vimified.com](https://www.vimified.com/)), vscodevimacademy logs speed and keypresses per task with no trend ([GitHub](https://github.com/kaisunc/vscodevimacademy)), VimGym shows a percentile per challenge and markets "visual progress charts" that its repo does not document ([GitHub](https://github.com/oller/vimgym)), and hardtime.nvim produces a report of your most-triggered bad habits without generating any drills ([GitHub](https://github.com/m4xshen/hardtime.nvim)).

The matrix shows two things. The in-editor niche that vim-be-good owned is vacant: the original has had **no push since December 2024** with a 2026 crash issue open, and its 25-game fork went quiet after three days of commits ([GitHub API](https://api.github.com/search/repositories?q=repo:ThePrimeagen/vim-be-good); [vim-be-better](https://github.com/szymonwilczek/vim-be-better)). And the free browser tier is crowded with a 2025–2026 wave of solo projects at 0–5 stars (VimGym, vimchi, vim-mastery, hjkl), so "interactive lessons" is no longer a differentiator. The empty slot is specifically measurement: per-motion latency and keystroke-efficiency ratio charted over sessions, driving which drill comes next.

User complaints confirm where the value is. Ranked by frequency across HN and review sites, the top grievances are time-limited licences (Vim Adventures' 6-month pass was called "$50. A year." in 2017 and Vimgolf.ai's $25 per 6 months was labelled "SaaS slop" within hours in 2026), courses that "don't go much beyond basics", emulator inaccuracies in Ace-based products such as "`:5d` doesn't delete line #5", and browser performance (Vimgolf.ai's real Neovim container drew "unholy amounts of CPU") ([HN 14656591](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=14656591); [HN 49095143](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=49095143); [HN 25846347](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=25846347)). The pricing anger is about the model, not the amount: $20–35 lifetime passes without comment every time. Users also ask for efficiency-based scoring rather than raw speed, content that continues into dot-repeat, registers, macros and `:g`/`:normal`, honest or absent leaderboards after Vim Racer's scripted 1-second entries, and a bridge to the real editor ("bring the move optimization advice into my actually coding environment") ([HN 41347051](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=41347051); [Product Hunt](https://www.producthunt.com/products/vim-adventures/reviews)). No source asks for "a keybr for Vim" by name; the need is assembled from these pieces.

## The keybr loop is four ideas and its paywall is ad removal

keybr's adaptive engine is open under AGPL-3.0 and small enough to read in an afternoon. Each key has a confidence score of `targetTime / EMA(timeToType)` with EMA alpha 0.1 and a default target of 175 CPM; the first six letters are always unlocked, the next unlocks once every included key has reached confidence 1.0, and every generated word must contain the single weakest unlocked key ([keybr-lesson/guided.ts](https://github.com/aradzie/keybr.com/blob/master/packages/keybr-lesson/lib/guided.ts); [keybr-result/keystats.ts](https://github.com/aradzie/keybr.com/blob/master/packages/keybr-result/lib/keystats.ts)). Progress prediction regresses speed against lesson index over the last 30 lessons, fits a linear, quadratic or cubic curve by sample count, projects up to 50 lessons ahead, and reports only when R² is at least 0.5 ([learningrate.ts](https://github.com/aradzie/keybr.com/blob/master/packages/keybr-lesson/lib/learningrate.ts)). The known weaknesses are that there is no minimum sample count and accuracy does not gate unlocking, so users game it ([velvet issue #5](https://github.com/pauldavidkelly/velvet/issues/5)). The AGPL matters: reusing the code in a hosted product triggers network copyleft, but the ideas are trivial to reimplement.

The port to Vim is direct. Treat each skill (`ciw`, `dap`, `f{char}`, `:g/pat/d`) as a "key", measure latency from challenge presentation to command completion, keep an EMA, define confidence as par time over EMA or par keystrokes over actual keystrokes, unlock new skills when all active ones hit confidence 1, and bias challenge generation toward the weakest skill. Add the two gates keybr lacks (minimum samples, accuracy). FSRS, whose stability-difficulty model has 21 trainable parameters in FSRS-6 and converges rather than scaling linearly on overdue reviews, is the right tool for scheduling re-tests of skills already mastered, with the grade derived from correctness, keystrokes versus par and time rather than self-report ([awesome-fsrs wiki](https://github.com/open-spaced-repetition/awesome-fsrs/wiki/The-Algorithm)). The Keystroke-Level Model's M operator (mental preparation) explains why latency beats keystroke count alone: Vim proficiency is largely the removal of the pause between operator and motion ([KLM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keystroke-level_model)). keybr's top user complaint, that "many of the words are made up", maps to contrived edit tasks, which argues for generating drills from real code snippets ([HN 31097516](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=31097516)).

The typing-trainer business model is just as instructive and less encouraging. Every consumer paywall in the category is "remove ads plus cosmetics plus data retention": TypeRacer Premium is $20/year for no ads, avatars and CSV export ([TypeRacer wiki](https://typeracer.fandom.com/wiki/Premium_Account)); Nitro Type Gold is $2.49–3.99 a month, randomized per account as a live price test, after the $19.99 annual plan was removed in December 2025 ([Nitro Type wiki](https://nitro.fandom.com/wiki/Gold_Membership)); Ratatype Plus is $49.99/year ([Ratatype](https://www.ratatype.com/ratatype-plus/)). Nobody gates the core learning loop. keybr itself sells only a one-time lifetime ad removal, reported at roughly **$13–14** by secondary sources, and the author spent 2015 to at least 2020 fielding requests before shipping it, having said in 2015 that he wanted "to give people a product in exchange for money" rather than a subscription ([keybr Google Group](https://groups.google.com/g/keybr/c/PsYcvOkJyWE); [Typing Monkey](https://typingmonkey.app/r/833/keybr-com-free)). The large operators make their real money from schools at $1–10 per seat per year with 20-seat minimums ([edclub licensing](https://s.typingclub.com/docs/faq/licensing-purchasing.html)).

Monkeytype is the structural analog for a solo developer and its numbers are sobering. One person runs it full time as his "primary job", it had **2M registered users and ~120,000 daily actives in May 2023**, and it is funded by Patreon, Ko-fi, merch and ads that users must opt in to ([kbd.news interview](https://kbd.news/Interview-3-years-of-Monkeytype-2019.html)). Its Patreon on 2026-10-11 showed **96 paying members and $167.30 a month** ([Patreon](https://www.patreon.com/monkeytype/about)), a donor conversion well under 0.1% of daily users. Whatever keeps its founder employed is therefore opt-in ads and merch at a scale of about 14 million visits a month, not donations. Across keybr, Monkeytype and 10FastFingers the single most-cited negative is ads that add input latency or layout shift in the practice area ([monkeytype issue 928](https://github.com/Miodec/monkeytype/issues/928); [keybr group](https://groups.google.com/g/keybr/c/PsYcvOkJyWE)). No typing trainer sells a ~$1 tier; the lowest consumer price found is Nitro Type's $2.49 a month.

## Ads pay $60–250 a month at 50k pageviews and $1 nets 45 cents

Developers are the most ad-blocked audience online. Stack Overflow's 2018 survey of 100,000+ developers found **72% use ad blockers**, and 2026 aggregators put developer-tool sites at 40–60% blocked, the highest of any vertical, against a general-population rate of about 30% ([B2B Marketing on SO data](https://www.b2bmarketing.net/majority-of-tech-developers-using-ad-blockers/); [Backlinko](https://backlinko.com/ad-blockers-users)). The two networks built for this audience pay modestly and gate entry. EthicalAds pays publishers 70% of what it charges, "most publishers on our network earn between $2.25 and $2.75 CPM", it requires **at least 50,000 pageviews per month**, insists on being the only ad on the page and above the fold, and counts an impression only after a full second in the viewport ([EthicalAds calculator](https://www.ethicalads.io/publishers/calculator/); [publisher FAQ](https://www.ethicalads.io/publishers/faq/)). Carbon Ads charges advertisers $6 CPM for developer audiences, is invitation-only, and does not disclose its publisher share; one aggregator estimates publishers see $0.50–1.10 CPM, a low-confidence figure ([Carbon media kit](https://www.carbonads.net/more-info); [monetizes.net](https://monetizes.net/networks/carbon-ads)). AdSense technology-niche page RPM runs $5–20 by one benchmark, and a programming-tutorial blog with ~100k monthly visits reported about $500 a month ([Techconda](https://www.techconda.com/2026/02/adsense-rpm-benchmarks.html); [eastondev](https://eastondev.com/blog/en/posts/media/20260108-adsense-high-cpc-niches/)).

Applying those inputs with 50% of pageviews unblocked (my arithmetic, not a sourced figure) gives the planning range below. A trainer is a single long-dwell page with keyboard focus, so it generates few pageviews per minute of use and should expect the bottom of each band.

| Monthly pageviews | EthicalAds | Carbon (unknown share) | AdSense ($5–20 RPM) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10,000 | below the 50k gate | ~$3–21 | ~$25–100 |
| 50,000 | ~$55–70 (up to ~$140 if their CPM already nets adblock) | ~$12–100 | ~$125–500 |
| 200,000 | ~$225–275 (up to ~$500) | ~$50–420 | ~$500–2,000 |

For scale, EthicalAds paid all of its publishers combined **$124,300 in January 2026** ([EthicalAds newsletter](https://www.ethicalads.io/blog/2026/04/ethicalads-newsletter-april-2026/)), so the average developer-network publisher earns on the order of hundreds of dollars a month. Reaching 50k pageviews is itself the hard part: keybr, a mature product in a far larger market, draws about 3.6M visits a month, and Vim users are a small slice of developers ([TypoTrainer citing Similarweb](https://typotrainer.lukadevv.com/blog/best-typing-practice-tools)).

The $1 tier fares worse because fixed per-transaction fees dominate. Stripe charges 2.9% + 30¢ and enforces a **$0.50 minimum charge** "to make sure the Stripe fee doesn't exceed your charge" ([Stripe pricing](https://stripe.com/pricing); [Stripe currencies](https://docs.stripe.com/currencies)). Merchants of record, which take on VAT and sales-tax liability, charge roughly 5% + 50¢ (Paddle, Polar Starter, Lemon Squeezy) and Paddle explicitly says sub-$10 sellers should "contact us for bespoke pricing" ([Paddle pricing](https://www.paddle.com/pricing); [Polar fees](https://polar.sh/docs/merchant-of-record/fees); [Lemon Squeezy fees](https://docs.lemonsqueezy.com/help/getting-started/fees)). Gumroad takes 10% + 50¢ ([Gumroad](https://gumroad.com/pricing)). The exceptions are PayPal Micropayments at 5% + 5¢, which requires PayPal's explicit approval, and GitHub Sponsors, which passes 100% of personal sponsorships through ([Checkoutpage](https://checkoutpage.com/blog/paypal-fees); [GitHub Docs](https://docs.github.com/en/sponsors/getting-started-with-github-sponsors/about-github-sponsors)).

| Processor | $1 nets | $5 nets | $10 nets |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stripe direct (2.9% + 30¢) | $0.67 | $4.56 | $9.41 |
| Paddle / Polar Starter / Lemon Squeezy (5% + 50¢, MoR) | $0.45 | $4.25 | $9.00 |
| Polar Pro (3.8% + 40¢, plus monthly fee) | $0.56 | $4.41 | $9.22 |
| Gumroad direct (10% + 50¢) | $0.40 | $4.00 | $8.50 |
| PayPal Micropayments (approval required) | $0.90 | $4.70 | $9.45 |
| GitHub Sponsors, personal | $1.00 | $5.00 | $10.00 |

The knee of the curve is at $4–5, where the fixed 30–50¢ falls below 10% of revenue; at $1 a merchant of record eats 55% of the sale. Combine that with conversion. Developer-facing freemium converts about **1–3% of free users** ([Monetizely on OpenView benchmarks](https://www.getmonetizely.com/articles/whats-the-right-ratio-of-free-to-paid-users-in-developer-saas)), so 1,000 monthly actives at $1 yield 10–30 sales and **$5–20 a month**; the same users at $5 yield $43–137. Donation buttons do worse still: a blog experiment saw 5 clicks and one $3 donation from 4,000 visitors, and an Indie Hackers report counted one Buy Me a Coffee donation per 4,206 visitors ([Shaun Poore](https://www.shaunpoore.com/conversion-rate-donation-buttons/); [Indie Hackers](https://www.indiehackers.com/post/1-4206-conversion-rate-on-buy-me-a-coffee-1ed21a2288)).

The deeper problem with $1 is what it tests. The only Vim product with disclosed revenue is vim.so: **$25 one-time, about $10,000 total by April 2021**, and the author reported that "when I raised the price, the conversion rate stayed pretty much exactly the same" ([HN 26825566](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=26825566)). Its successor LearnVim now charges $35 and claims 2,082 buyers ([learnvim.com](https://www.learnvim.com/)). The pay-what-you-want field experiment by Gneezy and colleagues found fewer people bought a photo when they could choose the price than when it cost a fixed $5, even though they could have paid $5 ([Gneezy et al.](https://marketing.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/Pay-What-You-Want-PAPER-Gneezy-Ayelet-4-12-2012.pdf)), and the anchoring literature shows a low advertised price reduces what people are later willing to pay ([ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/327164185_The_Influence_of_Pricing_Strategies_on_Willingness_to_Pay_for_Accommodations_Anchoring_Framing_and_Metric_Compatibility)). HN's 2025 thread on small web tools converged on the same view: "$5/month is a terrible price" and users prefer "one-time $20-$25 payments" ([HN 44019193](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=44019193)). A $1 tier measures willingness to type a card number, not willingness to pay, and it sets an anchor you will have to fight later. The disclosed small-dev-tool successes (Bear Blog, Zest UI, Mockoon, Quastor) cluster at $500–1,000 a month on $5–25 pricing ([HN 38935714](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=38935714)); none got there on ads alone or on a $1 price.

Tax is the final argument for a merchant of record once you do charge. A non-EU seller owes destination-country VAT on EU consumer digital sales **from the first euro**; the €10,000 threshold applies only to sellers established in one EU state ([Dodo Payments EU VAT guide](https://dodopayments.com/blogs/eu-vat-saas-guide-2026)). At tens to low hundreds of dollars a month the MoR premium is a few dollars and cheaper than quarterly OSS filings. Among MoRs, Lemon Squeezy is winding toward migration into Stripe Managed Payments after its 2024 acquisition, so Polar and Paddle are the stable choices today ([Paritydeals](https://www.paritydeals.com/stripe-managed-payments-vs-lemon-squeezy-fees/)).

## CodeMirror-vim in the browser, real Neovim offline, vimhjkl for content

For a solo developer the editor choice is settled: **CodeMirror 6 with `@replit/codemirror-vim`**, MIT-licensed, 38.5 KB gzipped for the keymap, 476 stars with 641 commits and an active issue tracker, and a public API that includes `Vim.handleKey(cm, key)` for programmatic replay, `Vim.defineEx` and `Vim.map` ([replit/codemirror-vim](https://github.com/replit/codemirror-vim); [bundlephobia](https://bundlephobia.com/api/size?package=@replit/codemirror-vim)). Its gaps are edge cases rather than core grammar: open issues cover `%` on some bracket pairs, `U`, `g@`/operatorfunc, Select mode, a macro-replay hang and some visual-mode cursor positioning ([issue tracker](https://github.com/replit/codemirror-vim/issues)). The practical consequence is to publish the supported-command list, which is also what users asked Vim Racer for. monaco-vim is a port of the same keymap with a less active upstream and a multi-megabyte Monaco peer dependency; the open-source course dofy/learning-vim already runs on the CodeMirror stack ([monaco-vim](https://github.com/brijeshb42/monaco-vim); [dofy/learning-vim](https://github.com/dofy/learning-vim)). Detecting command completion for latency measurement means watching for the return to normal mode with no pending operator or count; the engine exposes a mode-change event, but the exact event names should be verified in `packages/codemirror-vim-core` before relying on them.

Real Vim in the browser is not ready for the interactive loop. rhysd/vim.wasm compiles **Vim 8.2.0055**, its last npm release was December 2019, every commit since 2023 is a token renewal, and it requires SharedArrayBuffer with no mouse or IME ([rhysd/vim.wasm](https://github.com/rhysd/vim.wasm)). Neovim's own WASM target (issue #35567) is open and blocked on libuv, LuaJIT under Emscripten and the cross-compile toolchain as of May 2026 ([neovim#35567](https://github.com/neovim/neovim/issues/35567)). MuNeNICK/nvim-wasm does work as an unofficial wasm32-wasi build with msgpack-RPC access, but the binary is **7.8–8.4 MB** and needs COOP/COEP headers or Asyncify ([munenick.me](https://www.munenick.me/en/blog/nvim-wasm/)). Vimgolf.ai, the one browser product running real Neovim in a container, is the one that drew CPU and latency complaints. The right hybrid, which nobody has built, is codemirror-vim for the interactive UI and real Neovim only at build time to verify pars and expected outputs, exactly as vimhjkl does with `vim -W`. A Neovim 0.12 install already provides `nvim --headless` for that step.

Grading patterns are consistent across every system found: buffer equality first, keystroke count against par second, time as a tiebreaker or tracked metric ([vimgolf.com/about](https://www.vimgolf.com/about)). vimhjkl's refinements are worth copying wholesale: accept any solution within 2x par rather than matching a keystroke string, size buffers so that retyping the target in insert mode exceeds the par band, require that Ex-command drills actually ran an Ex command, grade motion-only drills by cursor target rather than buffer, and attach `why_not` notes explaining why naive alternatives fail ([vimhjkl CONTRIBUTING](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/S-Sigdel/vimhjkl/main/CONTRIBUTING.md)). Vim Mastery computes optimal motion paths with Dijkstra search through its engine ([Ichihiroy/vim-mastery](https://github.com/Ichihiroy/vim-mastery)); normalise keystrokes in VimGolf's angle-bracket notation so `<Esc>` counts as one key.

Content licensing splits three ways. **vimhjkl's 66 skills and 230 challenges are MIT**, contributors agree their work falls under MIT, every challenge is machine-verified, and it is the only open set tagged by skill, so it is the one clearly reusable seed with attribution ([vimhjkl LICENSE](https://github.com/S-Sigdel/vimhjkl/blob/main/LICENSE)). The caveat is that part of its pool is LLM-generated, which raises a copyrightability question no source resolves and which drew disclosure criticism on Reddit. **VimGolf's software is MIT but its 621 challenge texts are user-submitted content on vimgolf.com with no terms of use or licence anywhere on the site**, so treat them as not reusable; the "unlicense" label on the Hugging Face scrape was applied by the scraper, not the authors ([VimGolf LICENSE](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/igrigorik/vimgolf/master/LICENSE); [vimgolf.com/about](https://www.vimgolf.com/about); [Hugging Face](https://huggingface.co/datasets/James4Ever0/vimgolf_challenges_and_solutions)). The **vimtutor text ships under the Vim licence**, whose modification clauses require notices and source-sharing or GPL v2+, so rewrite rather than copy ([vim/vim LICENSE](https://github.com/vim/vim/blob/master/LICENSE)). Dynamically generated challenges, as Vim Hero and Vim Mastery do, sidestep both the licensing question and the memorisation problem of fixed puzzles, and generating them from real code snippets answers keybr's "made-up words" complaint.

## Verdict: drop ads and the $1 tier, sell a $20 lifetime pro

**The ads plus $1 plan fails on both halves, and it fails as a test, not just as a business.** Ads need 50k pageviews a month to clear EthicalAds' gate, and a one-page keyboard-focused trainer produces few pageviews per session, so the realistic outcome in year one is zero ad income or a Carbon/AdSense placement earning tens of dollars while degrading the one thing users in this category complain about most, input latency in the practice area. The $1 tier nets 45–67 cents, sits at Stripe's $0.50 floor, has no precedent in either the typing-trainer or Vim-course markets, and anchors the product's perceived value at a price you cannot later raise without the "SaaS slop" reaction the niche reserves for anything that smells like a subscription. Most importantly it does not produce the signal you want. Ten sales at $1 from 1,000 users tells you ten people were willing to enter a card; vim.so's experience is that the same people pay $25 at the same rate.

The alternative with precedent is a three-layer model. The core trainer is free, with no ads at all in the practice loop; if ads ever appear, they are opt-in on the results page in Monkeytype's style and only after traffic clears the EthicalAds gate. A **one-time "pro" purchase at $15–25** sold through Polar or Paddle unlocks the things users have shown they value and that cost you something: history beyond a rolling window, per-skill charts and learning-rate projection, cross-device sync, data export, and the advanced curriculum (registers, macros, `:g`/`:normal`, regex substitution). That price mirrors keybr's ~$13–14 lifetime, Vim Hero's ~$20, Vimified's $20 and vim.so's $25, all of which pass without pricing complaints, and it clears the fee knee so a merchant of record keeps 90%. A GitHub Sponsors link costs nothing and nets 100% from the small fraction who want to tip. If you want a real price test, run it between $15 and $29 one-time once there are a few hundred monthly actives, which is the only range where the niche has data. Do not add payment to the MVP at all: the first milestone is engaged free users and a per-skill chart people screenshot, and Show HN plus r/neovim (where vimhjkl drew ~479 points) are the launch channels that reach them.

The MVP scope follows from the gaps. Ship a single page on CodeMirror 6 plus `@replit/codemirror-vim` with a published supported-command list, configurable relative numbers and arrow-key policy, and colourblind-safe target highlighting. Cover **20–30 skills across motions, operators and text objects** (roughly Vim Hero's first five categories), each with a generator that produces challenges from real code snippets requiring the focus skill, graded by buffer equality or cursor target within a 2x par band, with pars verified against real Neovim headless at build time. Record per-skill latency and keystroke ratio per attempt, keep an EMA-based confidence, gate unlocks on all active skills reaching confidence 1 with a minimum sample count and accuracy floor, pick the focus skill as the weakest active one, and chart every skill's confidence over sessions with a keybr-style lessons-remaining projection. Persist to localStorage with import/export first; accounts and sync are a pro feature, which keeps the MVP free of auth and database work. Leave out leaderboards entirely. Seed the exercise pool from vimhjkl's MIT set with attribution where its skill tags match, and write everything else fresh. A companion Neovim plugin that feeds real-editing telemetry back into the skill model, the inverse of the "bring the advice into my coding environment" request, is the clearest second-phase differentiator and the one Vim Hero cannot easily copy from a browser.

## Conclusion

The research reframes the question from "is there room for another Vim trainer" to "why has nobody measured anything." Thirty products teach the same hundred commands with the same lesson-list shape, and the two that come closest to adaptivity each hold half of the answer: Vim Hero has the browser and the per-command ladder, vimhjkl has weakness-driven scheduling and real-Vim verification. The combination is small enough for one person because the hard algorithmic part is already written down in keybr's source, the hard editor part is an MIT package, and the hard verification part is `nvim --headless` on a machine that already runs Neovim 0.12. The moat is not the code; it is the accumulated per-skill telemetry that makes the charts and the drill selection better than a fixed curriculum, and that telemetry only exists once people use the thing.

The monetization finding is that this niche has already run the pricing experiment for you, repeatedly, over fifteen years. Time-limited licences get attacked, lifetime prices between $13 and $35 do not, donations convert below a tenth of a percent even for beloved products, and ads only matter at traffic levels a Vim trainer is unlikely to reach. Treating a $1 tier as a cheap test misreads what is cheap: the expensive part of any paid tier is the entitlement, tax and support plumbing, which costs the same at $1 as at $20, while the $1 figure throws away the only signal that plumbing could have produced.
